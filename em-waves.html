01 · Maxwell's equations
01 — Foundation
Maxwell's Equations
Four coupled PDEs governing all classical electrodynamics — Faraday, Ampere, Gauss, and Gauss for magnetism.
→
E and H field — mutually generating, TEM wave propagation

The four equations

Maxwell's equations are the most general mathematical statement of classical electrodynamics. They describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated by charges and currents, and how they interact with each other.

LawDifferential formPhysical meaning
Faraday$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$Time-varying B produces E
Ampere (generalized)$\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t}$Currents and time-varying D produce H
Gauss (electric)$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho$Charge is the source of D
Gauss (magnetic)$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$No magnetic monopoles — B loops are closed
Maxwell's key contribution

The displacement current term $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ in Ampere's Law was purely theoretical — it predicts that a time-varying E field generates H even without conduction current. This is what allows EM waves to propagate through free space where $\mathbf{J} = 0$. Without it, EM waves cannot exist.

Wave generation — the feedback loop

The two curl equations work together in a self-sustaining cycle: a time-varying H field generates an E field (Faraday), and that time-varying E field regenerates an H field (Ampere). This process repeats, propagating energy outward as a wave — no medium needed.

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\mu\frac{\partial \mathbf{H}}{\partial t}$$
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \epsilon\frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$$

Constitutive relations

In simple media the field quantities are linked through material constants:

  • $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon \mathbf{E}$ — permittivity $\epsilon$ ties electric flux density to E-field
  • $\mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{H}$ — permeability $\mu$ ties magnetic flux to H-field
  • $\mathbf{J}_c = \sigma \mathbf{E}$ — conductivity $\sigma$ ties conduction current to E-field (Ohm's law in point form)
02 · Material properties
02 — Media
Material Properties in EM
Permittivity, permeability, and conductivity — how materials store, concentrate, and dissipate EM energy.
→

Permittivity ε

Permittivity measures how much electric energy a material can store. Analogous to capacitance in circuit theory ($C = Q/V$). In a dielectric, atoms and molecules polarize under an applied E-field, creating a macroscopic polarization vector P:

$$\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0 \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\mathbf{E} = \epsilon \mathbf{E}$$

The relative permittivity $\epsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e \geq 1$ for all dielectrics, since polarization always enhances flux density. For glass, $\epsilon_r \approx 4$, which slows the wave to $v_p = c/2$.

Permeability μ

Permeability measures how much magnetic energy a material can store — analogous to inductance ($L = \Phi/I$). Magnetic materials develop a magnetization vector M:

$$\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}) = \mu_0\mu_r \mathbf{H}$$

Unlike $\epsilon_r$, the susceptibility $\chi_m$ can be negative (diamagnetic materials), giving $\mu_r < 1$.

Conductivity σ

Conductivity describes how freely charge carriers move. Higher $\sigma$ means more conduction current for the same applied E-field, dissipating energy as Joule heat. This is Ohm's law in point form: $\mathbf{J}_c = \sigma\mathbf{E}$.

Free space constants

  • $\epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m — permittivity of free space
  • $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m — permeability of free space
  • $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0} \approx 3\times10^8$ m/s — speed of light in vacuum

D and H vs. E and B

  • D and H depend only on free charges and free currents — they are material-independent in that sense
  • E and B include material response and directly describe force and energy storage
Simple medium assumption

A simple medium is linear (response proportional to field), homogeneous (properties don't vary with position), and isotropic (properties don't depend on field direction). This lets us use phasor (time-harmonic) analysis, which is the foundation of all RF/microwave circuit theory.

03 · Time-harmonic form
03 — Phasors
Time-Harmonic (Phasor) Form
Steady-state sinusoidal analysis — replacing ∂/∂t with jω to turn PDEs into algebraic equations.
→

The phasor representation

For time-harmonic (sinusoidal steady-state) fields, we represent the instantaneous field as the real part of a complex phasor multiplied by $e^{j\omega t}$:

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \text{Re}\!\left[\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r})\, e^{j\omega t}\right]$$

The phasor $\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r})$ is complex-valued and carries magnitude and phase but no time dependence. The key simplification: time derivatives become multiplication by $j\omega$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \longrightarrow j\omega$$

Maxwell's equations in phasor form

LawInstantaneousPhasor
Faraday$\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$$\nabla\times\bar{\mathbf{E}} = -j\omega\mu\bar{\mathbf{H}}$
Ampere$\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$$\nabla\times\bar{\mathbf{H}} = (\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)\bar{\mathbf{E}}$
Gauss (E)$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$$\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathbf{D}} = \rho$
Gauss (B)$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$$\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathbf{B}} = 0$
Why this matters for RF

Every RF circuit analysis tool — S-parameters, impedance matching, Smith charts, network analyzers — operates in the phasor (frequency) domain. The phasor form is what makes all of these tractable. VNAs measure $\bar{S}_{ij}(\omega)$ directly.

04 · Wave equation & plane waves
04 — Propagation
Wave Equation & Plane Waves
Helmholtz equation, phase velocity, wavelength, intrinsic impedance, and the TEM nature of plane waves.
→
TEM plane wave — E (cyan) and H (violet) fields perpendicular, propagating in z

Deriving the wave equation

Take the curl of Faraday's Law, apply Ampere's Law to eliminate H, and use $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=0$ (source-free). The result is the homogeneous wave equation (Helmholtz equation):

$$\nabla^2\bar{\mathbf{E}} + \beta^2\bar{\mathbf{E}} = 0 \qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$$

Plane wave solution

For a uniform plane wave traveling in the $+z$ direction, only $E_x$ and $E_y$ components exist ($E_z = 0$ by Gauss's law). The solution is:

$$E_x(z,t) = C_1\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$$

Key parameters

  • β — phase constant (rad/m): $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$
  • λ — wavelength: distance over which phase changes by $2\pi$ rad
  • vₚ — phase velocity: $v_p = \omega/\beta = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = c/\sqrt{\mu_r\epsilon_r}$
  • η — intrinsic impedance: ratio of E to H magnitudes, units of Ω

Intrinsic impedance

From Faraday's Law applied to the plane wave solution, the ratio of E to H magnitudes is fixed by the medium:

$$\eta = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\epsilon_0}} \approx 377\,\Omega \text{ (free space)}$$

TEM waves

Plane waves are Transverse Electromagnetic (TEM) — both E and H are perpendicular to the direction of propagation, and perpendicular to each other. The direction of propagation is given by $\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}$ (Poynting vector direction).

Phase analysis

The phase of the wave is $(\omega t - \beta z)$. The $\omega t$ term advances phase with time; the $\beta z$ term retards it with distance. To ride a constant-phase surface (move with the wave), you must move at $v_p = \omega/\beta$. A phase shift of $\phi = \beta d$ accumulates over distance $d$ — this is exactly what a VNA measures as $\angle S_{21}$.

05 · Boundary conditions
05 — Interfaces
Boundary Conditions
How E and H field components behave at material interfaces — the rules governing reflection and refraction.
→
Boundary interface — tangential E continuous, normal D discontinuous at surface charge

Derivation approach

Apply the integral form of Maxwell's equations to an infinitesimally thin region straddling the interface: a thin rectangular loop for curl equations (tangential components), a flat "pillbox" cylinder for divergence equations (normal components), then take the height to zero.

Results

ComponentGeneral conditionDielectric–dielectric ($\rho_s=0$, $J_s=0$)
Tangential E$E_{1t} = E_{2t}$Continuous always
Normal D$D_{1n} - D_{2n} = \rho_s$$D_{1n} = D_{2n}$ (continuous)
Tangential H$H_{1t} - H_{2t} = J_s$$H_{1t} = H_{2t}$ (continuous)
Normal B$B_{1n} = B_{2n}$Continuous always
Why this matters

Boundary conditions are what cause reflections at impedance discontinuities in transmission lines, waveguides, and antenna apertures. At a perfect conductor, $E_t = 0$ on the surface (tangential E must be zero), which forces a reflected wave that cancels the tangential field — this is why metals are good reflectors.

06 · Lossy media
06 — Attenuation
Waves in Lossy Media
Complex propagation constant, attenuation, skin effect, loss tangent, and classifying conductors vs. dielectrics.
→
Attenuated plane wave — exponential amplitude decay e^(−αz) in lossy medium

Two loss mechanisms

  • Conduction loss — free charges driven by E-field create current; resistance dissipates energy as Joule heat ($J_c = \sigma E$)
  • Dielectric loss — time-varying E-field forces polar molecules to reorient; molecular friction dissipates energy; polarization P lags E

Complex permittivity

The lagging polarization makes susceptibility complex, giving a complex permittivity:

$$\epsilon_c = \epsilon' - j\epsilon'' \qquad \epsilon' = \text{energy storage}, \quad \epsilon'' = \text{dielectric loss}$$

Complex propagation constant

Substituting into the Helmholtz equation gives $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ where both parts are now nonzero:

$$\bar{E}(z) = \bar{E}_0\,e^{-\gamma z} = \bar{E}_0\,e^{-\alpha z}\,e^{-j\beta z}$$
  • α — attenuation constant (Np/m): controls exponential amplitude decay
  • β — phase constant (rad/m): controls oscillation phase

Loss tangent

The effective loss tangent compares total loss current to displacement current — it classifies how lossy a material is:

$$\tan\delta_\text{eff} = \frac{\sigma_\text{eff}}{\omega\epsilon'} = \frac{\sigma + \omega\epsilon''}{\omega\epsilon'}$$
ClassificationConditionBehavior
Good dielectric$\tan\delta \ll 1$Small α — wave propagates with slight attenuation
Good conductor$\tan\delta \gg 1$Large α — wave attenuates rapidly

Skin depth

For a good conductor, $\alpha \approx \beta \approx \sqrt{\omega\mu\sigma/2}$. The skin depth $\delta_s$ is the distance over which amplitude decays to $1/e \approx 37\%$:

$$\delta_s = \frac{1}{\alpha} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}$$

At 1 GHz, skin depth in copper is only ~2 μm. This means RF currents flow only in a thin skin near the conductor surface — the skin effect. This is why surface roughness matters at microwave frequencies, and why your PCB copper finish matters above ~1 GHz.

Complex intrinsic impedance

In a lossy medium, $\eta_c = |\eta_c|e^{j\theta_\eta}$ is complex. This means E and H are no longer in time phase — H lags E by angle $\theta_\eta$. Analogous to an AC circuit with complex impedance where voltage and current are out of phase.
